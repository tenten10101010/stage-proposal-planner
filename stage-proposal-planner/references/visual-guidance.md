# Visual Guidance for Stage Proposals

Use this reference when the proposal will become a PPT, visual deck, image-led page, or visually polished report.

## Start With the Viewing Job

Decide whether the page must:

- create emotion;
- explain structure;
- show evidence;
- invite cooperation;
- close the proposal.

One page should not do all of these.

## Text and Background

Keep critical text on clean surfaces:

- warm white;
- deep navy;
- soft muted color planes;
- simple paper or UI surfaces.

Do not place Chinese text over faces, animals, dense screenshots, complex linework, dark brush strokes, or busy backgrounds. This is different from putting every sentence in a box.

## Use a Visual Mother, Not a Fixed Template

Maintain recognizable continuity through:

- color family;
- typography mood;
- line quality;
- margins and breathing;
- recurring motifs such as thread, paper, light, archive, or system lines.

Avoid using the same left-text/right-image layout on every page. At section breaks, vary the composition:

- centered manifesto;
- three-column capability page;
- horizontal timeline;
- relationship map;
- product-system display;
- interview archive;
- abstract asset constellation;
- closing hero.

## Color Rhythm

Do not let a long deck become only navy, gold, and cream. Keep the base system stable, but allow each subchapter to own a controlled accent palette.

Useful restrained accents:

- warm terracotta for personal story and emotional warmth;
- mist green for community, care, and continuity;
- tea brown for archive, interview, and paper texture;
- muted lavender-gray for memory, reflection, and complexity;
- soft slate blue for professional or evidence-based pages.

Use accents as washes, tags, thread nodes, small surfaces, or silhouette shadows. Do not convert the whole page into a new color system.

## Diagrams

When showing a project system:

- keep major nodes on one baseline when possible;
- use line color and style to show different meanings;
- place growth loops as secondary content if they are not the main argument;
- avoid dense arrows and equal-weight boxes.

Recommended line semantics:

- solid navy: asset accumulation or main structure;
- colored arrows: resource flow;
- dotted line: collaboration, sharing, or feedback loop;
- gold thread: long-term continuity or value transfer.

## Screenshots and Product Interfaces

When showing a mini-program, app, report, or product system:

- place screenshots inside phone or device frames;
- use two or three screens, not a raw screenshot collage;
- crop to the parts that prove the point;
- pair UI with a mechanism statement;
- show the loop from user action to evidence to next decision.

Do not let screenshots become the whole slide unless the user asks for UI review.

## People, Animals, and Portraits

Use people, animals, or character scenes only when they carry the page's meaning. If many previous pages already use similar relationship scenes, switch to:

- objects;
- abstract systems;
- silhouettes;
- interview notes;
- device mockups;
- data/evidence fields;
- relationship maps.

For unapproved interview subjects, use side profiles, silhouettes, or abstract role markers. Avoid identifiable likenesses.

## Expressing Assets

Do not default to a book, folder, or certificate for every asset. Choose based on the asset type:

- evidence asset: report stack, dashboard, structured record, data loop;
- content asset: story archive, annotated notes, editorial spread;
- relationship asset: constellation, network, layered light field, connected nodes;
- brand asset: seal, heritage light, quality thread, trust field;
- cooperation asset: invitation system, shared table, partner doorway.

For intangible assets, prefer an abstract constellation or layered field over a literal object.

## Batch Generation Discipline

When generating multiple visuals:

1. Keep the storyline and exact page purposes fixed.
2. Generate independent pages, not a contact sheet, if text quality matters.
3. Keep continuity in border, type mood, and palette.
4. Vary composition at subchapter changes.
5. After each batch, classify pages as keep, revise text, revise image, or redo.

Speed must not flatten the deck into one repetitive template.
